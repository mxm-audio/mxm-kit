# 12. FM and phase modulation

Read as an oscillator, FM is the odd one out in this reference. Every other technique here produces
a waveform with a bandwidth fixed by its shape — infinite, for anything with a corner in it — and
the work goes into limiting that bandwidth without ruining the sound. FM produces a waveform whose
**bandwidth is a knob**. One parameter takes it from a pure sine to something wider than the
sampling rate can hold.

That is the whole technique, and both halves of it matter: the reason FM sounds the way it does,
and the reason it aliases the way it does, are the same fact.

## 12.1 What the knob does

Chowning's 1973 result: modulate a carrier at `fc` with a sinusoid at `fm`, and the output is

```
y(t) = Σ_k  J_k(I) · sin(2π(fc + k·fm)t)
```

— sidebands at `fc ± k·fm`, spaced by the modulating frequency, with amplitudes given by the Bessel
functions `J_k(I)` of the **modulation index** `I = Δf / fm`.

Three consequences, all of them audible:

**Bandwidth grows with index.** As `I` rises, energy is, in Chowning's phrase, "stolen" from the
carrier and distributed among an increasing number of side frequencies. The standard estimate is
Carson's rule — roughly `I + 1` significant sideband pairs, so a total width of `2(I+1)·fm`.

**The carrier is not special.** At `I = 4`, `J_0(4) = −0.397` while `J_3(4) = 0.430`: the third
sideband is louder than the carrier. This is why the "fundamental" of an FM patch is often not the
carrier frequency, and why a peak-picking pitch detector run over FM output reports whatever
sideband happens to be winning. It caught this harness out — an early version of §12.2's
measurement reported a 1900-cent pitch shift that was really just the loudest partial moving.

**Negative frequencies fold, with a sign.** Sidebands at `fc − k·fm` go below zero once `k·fm > fc`.
Chowning: these components "reflect around 0 Hz and 'mix' with the components in the positive
domain" — and because the odd-order Bessel coefficients carry a negative sign, they can subtract as
easily as add. In his worked example (`c = 100`, `m = 100`, `I = 4`) the reflected third lower
sideband "will add to the first upper-side frequency with unlike signs, decreasing" it. So the
spectrum of an FM patch is not the textbook picture with a few bars; it is that picture folded onto
itself twice — once at 0 Hz, and again at Nyquist.

## 12.2 Everybody implements phase modulation, and they are right to

The DX7 does not frequency-modulate anything. It adds the modulator to the **index into a sine
table** — phase modulation — and this is the standard implementation everywhere.

For a sinusoidal modulator the two are identical: a peak phase deviation of `I` radians is a peak
frequency deviation of `I·fm`, so the spectra match exactly. They stop being identical the moment
the modulator has a non-zero mean, which is the normal case as soon as there is a modulator stack,
an offset envelope, or a feedback path.

Measured, with a modulator that is a sine plus a constant. `mean carrier` is the carrier's unwrapped
phase advance divided by elapsed time — what its frequency actually was, rather than which peak was
loudest:

| kind | modulator offset | mean carrier | cents off | alias |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| phase modulation | 0.0 | 440.76 Hz | 0.0 | −247.9 dB |
| phase modulation | **0.3** | **440.76 Hz** | **0.0** | −248.1 dB |
| true FM | 0.0 | 440.79 Hz | 0.1 | −248.0 dB |
| true FM | **0.3** | **969.70 Hz** | **+1365.1** | **+300 dB** |

True FM integrates the modulator, so a constant offset `d` becomes a permanent frequency shift of
`I·fm·d` — predicted `440.8 + 4 × 440.8 × 0.3 = 969.8 Hz`, measured 969.70. Phase modulation
integrates nothing: a constant offset is a constant phase offset, inaudible, and the measured
carrier does not move at all.

The alias column makes the second half of the point. Under PM the signal stays exactly periodic at
the fundamental, so the analysis of [chapter 1](01-fundamentals.md#15-the-measurement-method) finds
nothing off the harmonic grid. Under true FM the whole spectrum has slid to a non-harmonic position,
so essentially all of its energy is off-grid — the +300 dB is not a measurement of aliasing but of
the harmonic bins having been vacated.

**So: use phase modulation.** It is the same technique with one failure mode removed, it costs
nothing extra, and it is what the instruments everyone is emulating actually did.

There is a second, smaller reason. True FM's instantaneous frequency `fc + I·fm·m(t)` goes negative
for part of every cycle once `I·fm > fc`, so the phase runs backwards — legal, but it means every
wrap has to handle both directions, and any code that assumes a monotonic phase (a BLEP residual,
a wavetable mip selector, a sync detector) is now wrong.

## 12.3 Aliasing: a cliff, not a slope

Here is where FM behaves unlike everything else in this reference. A sawtooth aliases at every
pitch, by an amount that degrades smoothly ([chapter 2](02-antialiasing.md#211-the-comparison-table)).
FM is **clean until it isn't**.

Measured, carrier 440.8 Hz, phase modulation. `bw` is the highest bin within 60 dB of the peak;
`carson` is `fc + (I+1)·fm`; Nyquist is 22050 Hz:

| ratio | index | alias | vs 8× reference | bw | Carson |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1 | 1 | −247.7 dB | −115.0 dB | 2204 Hz | 1322 Hz |
| 1:1 | 2 | −247.9 dB | −115.3 dB | 3085 Hz | 1763 Hz |
| 1:1 | 4 | −247.9 dB | −115.2 dB | 4408 Hz | 2645 Hz |
| 1:1 | 8 | −248.7 dB | −115.5 dB | 6611 Hz | 4408 Hz |
| 1:1 | 16 | −249.2 dB | −113.5 dB | 10578 Hz | 7934 Hz |
| 1:1 | **32** | −132.2 dB | **−83.4 dB** | 18512 Hz | 14986 Hz |
| 1:1 | **64** | −1.3 dB | **−1.7 dB** | 22038 Hz | 29090 Hz |
| 1:8 | 1 | −113.4 dB | −113.1 dB | 14545 Hz | 7493 Hz |
| 1:8 | **2** | −72.1 dB | **−75.0 dB** | 21597 Hz | 11019 Hz |
| 1:8 | 4 | −33.1 dB | −35.8 dB | 21597 Hz | 18071 Hz |
| 1:8 | **8** | −2.8 dB | **−2.4 dB** | 21597 Hz | 32175 Hz |
| 1:8 | 64 | 11.5 dB | 12.2 dB | 21742 Hz | 229635 Hz |

The `vs reference` column bottoms out around **−115 dB**, which is the decimation filter's floor and
not a property of any oscillator — an unmodulated carrier measures the same. Read −115 as "no
measurable aliasing".

What the table says:

**Nothing happens, and then everything happens.** At 1:1 the output is measurably perfect up to
index 16 and destroyed by index 64. Two doublings of one parameter cover the entire range from
transparent to unusable. There is no setting where FM aliases "a little" for long.

**The predictor is bandwidth against Nyquist**, exactly as theory says. The −60 dB bandwidth runs
about **1.2 to 1.7 times Carson's estimate** (1.67× at index 1, 1.23× at index 32), so a useful
working rule is *budget 1.5 × Carson and keep it under Nyquist*.

**Ratio matters far more than index.** At 1:8, aliasing is already audible at index 2 — a setting no
one would think of as extreme — because `fm` is eight times larger and every sideband step is eight
times wider. High-ratio operators are where FM patches alias, not high-index ones.

**And all of it scales with pitch.** Every frequency in Carson's rule is proportional to the note,
so a patch that measures −115 dB at A2 measures −35 dB at A5. This is the reason DX-era instruments
have **key scaling** on operator output level: turning the index down as the note goes up is not a
tone control, it is the antialiasing strategy, implemented in 1983 as a per-operator envelope
offset.

## 12.4 Oversampling is the right answer here — and only here

[Chapter 2 §2.9](02-antialiasing.md#29-oversampling) concluded that oversampling is the worst deal
on the table for classic waveforms: 8× with a long filter buys 6 dB less than a four-point
polynomial at three thousand times the cost.

For FM the same measurement comes out the other way. At 1:8, index 8 — the setting that measured
−2.4 dB above — every row compared against the same 16× reference:

| rate | vs reference |
|---|---|
| 1× | −2.4 dB |
| **2×** | **−126.7 dB** |
| 4× | −174.2 dB |
| 8× | −199.6 dB |

**One doubling of the sample rate buys 124 dB.** Against 6 dB per doubling for a sawtooth.

The reason is the shape of the spectrum being folded. A sawtooth's harmonics decay at 6 dB/octave
forever, so there is always plenty of energy just above Nyquist to fold down, and doubling the rate
only moves the fold point one octave up a shallow slope. FM's sidebands are Bessel functions, and
`J_k(I)` collapses **super-exponentially** once `k > I` — past the Carson bandwidth there is
essentially nothing left. Double the rate and the fold point lands in that nothing.

Two caveats before treating this as a licence:

- **It is per-setting, not per-patch.** The 124 dB is for one index and ratio. A patch that automates
  the index — which is what an FM envelope is — has to be judged at its worst moment, and the worst
  moment of an FM envelope is usually the attack.
- **The cheaper fix is to not generate it.** Key scaling and an index limit that falls with pitch
  cost nothing per sample and were good enough for the instruments that defined the sound. Reach
  for 2× when the patch genuinely needs the bandwidth; reach for key scaling first.

## 12.5 Ratio, harmonicity, and where the aliasing actually comes from

Integer `c:m` ratios give a harmonic spectrum: every sideband `fc ± k·fm` is a multiple of
`f0 = fc/c = fm/m`, including the ones that reflect through zero. Non-integer ratios give
inharmonic spectra — the bell and metal timbres FM is known for.

Measured at index 4, all sharing a 55.1 Hz fundamental:

| c:m | fc | fm | alias | vs reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1 | 446.8 Hz | 446.8 Hz | −247.9 dB | −115.0 dB |
| 1:2 | 446.8 Hz | 893.6 Hz | −248.4 dB | −115.9 dB |
| 2:1 | 893.6 Hz | 446.8 Hz | −248.1 dB | −115.1 dB |
| 1:3 | 446.8 Hz | 1340.4 Hz | −187.5 dB | −114.1 dB |
| 3:2 | 1340.4 Hz | 893.6 Hz | −248.9 dB | −115.2 dB |
| 8:9 | 3574.5 Hz | 4021.3 Hz | −16.8 dB | −18.0 dB |
| 1:14 | 446.8 Hz | 6255.4 Hz | −6.1 dB | −5.6 dB |

The bottom two rows look like an indictment of odd ratios and are nothing of the kind — they are
§12.3 again. `8:9` puts both operators near 4 kHz, and `1:14` has a 6.25 kHz modulator; in both
cases Carson's bandwidth is far past Nyquist at index 4. **Inharmonicity does not cause aliasing.
Bandwidth does, and unusual ratios tend to come with large modulator frequencies.**

One thing worth noticing in the 1:3 row: its alias figure is −187 dB rather than −248. A 1:3 ratio
puts sidebands only on every third harmonic, so the remaining bins are empty and even a trace of
energy in them shows up. Sparse spectra make the measurement more sensitive, not the oscillator
worse.

## 12.6 Feedback

An operator modulating itself. The DX7 does this with a **two-sample average** in the feedback path,
and the reason is the one from [`../filters/`](../filters/README.md): a feedback loop with a unit
delay standing in for a delay-free path breaks into oscillation much earlier than the real circuit
would. Averaging the last two outputs damps it.

Measured, self-modulating at 440.8 Hz. `tilt` is the least-squares slope over the first eight
harmonics — a sawtooth is −6 dB/octave:

| feedback | vs reference | bandwidth | tilt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | −115.1 dB | 441 Hz | n/a (a sine has no harmonics to fit) |
| 0.25 | −63.5 dB | 2204 Hz | −31.20 dB/oct |
| 0.50 | −45.3 dB | 3526 Hz | −18.54 dB/oct |
| 1.00 | −21.3 dB | 9697 Hz | −9.82 dB/oct |
| 2.00 | −16.4 dB | 22038 Hz | −7.25 dB/oct |
| **3.00** | +3.8 dB | 22038 Hz | **−6.96 dB/oct** |
| 4.00 | +4.1 dB | 22049 Hz | −3.90 dB/oct |

**Feedback FM converges on a sawtooth.** By a feedback of 2–3 the spectral tilt is −7.25 to
−6.96 dB/octave, which is a sawtooth to within measurement error. This is the standard trick for
getting a saw out of an FM synth that only has sine operators, and the measurement confirms it does
what the folklore says.

**It also aliases from the very first step.** −63.5 dB at a feedback of 0.25, −21.3 dB at 1.0. For
comparison, the shipped two-point PolyBLEP sawtooth measures −35.4 dB at the same pitch. So
feedback FM is a *worse* sawtooth than the cheapest correction function, at 8.74 ns/sample against
1.58 — but it is a sawtooth with a continuously variable tilt, which no BLEP oscillator offers.

**Past a feedback of about 3 it stops being periodic at all.** The deviation figure goes positive,
the tilt collapses, and the bandwidth pins to Nyquist: the loop has gone chaotic. That is a
legitimate noise source and an illegitimate oscillator, and which one you have depends on a
parameter a user can sweep — so clamp it, or document it as a noise mode.

## 12.7 Cost

Mean of three runs, against the 0.54 ns/sample bare counter:

| variant | ns/sample | vs counter |
|---|---|---|
| FM pair, phase modulation | 22.08 | 41× |
| FM pair, true FM | 16.08 | 30× |
| single feedback operator | 8.74 | 16× |
| six-operator stack | 142.12 | 263× |

About **8 ns per operator**, which is one `sin()` — the 7.4 ns measured in
[11-additive-resynthesis.md §11.2](11-additive-resynthesis.md#112-three-ways-to-make-a-sine-measured).
FM's cost is entirely the transcendental, and a table-read sine halves it (3.7 ns per partial in the
same measurement). Everything else — the phase accumulators, the index multiply, the algorithm
routing — is noise next to it.

For scale: a six-operator voice is 142 ns/sample, about **0.7% of one core** at 48 kHz, so
sixteen-voice polyphony is around 11%. FM is cheap. It was not cheap in 1983, which is why the DX7
went to remarkable lengths to avoid multiplying at all: a **log-sine ROM of 1024 14-bit values**
covering a quarter cycle, delta-encoded down to 5344 bits, so that applying an envelope was an
*addition* in the log domain, with a pair of exponential ROMs to convert back at the end.

## 12.8 FM is not only an oscillator

The framing so far has been "FM as a waveform generator". Three other readings are worth having.

**FM is a modulation primitive, and it does not compose with the rest of this reference.** Any
oscillator can in principle be phase-modulated, and almost none of the ones in this document survive
it. Every correction function in [chapter 2](02-antialiasing.md) is derived for a phase that
advances by a constant `dt`; phase modulation makes the effective increment vary sample to sample,
so the residual's assumption about the slope either side of a discontinuity is wrong, and a
wavetable's mip selection — chosen from the nominal pitch — is wrong too. **This is the real reason
FM synthesizers use sine carriers**, and not merely tradition: a sine has no discontinuity to
band-limit, so there is nothing for the modulation to invalidate. We have not measured what a
phase-modulated PolyBLEP sawtooth does; it is recorded as an open gap in
[07-rust-recipes.md §7.6](07-rust-recipes.md#76-open-gaps).

**FM is waveshaping in disguise.** A self-modulating operator is `sin(x + a·sin(x))`, which is a
memoryless nonlinearity applied to a sine — a waveshaper, with everything that implies, including
the antiderivative antialiasing of [chapter 2 §2.10](02-antialiasing.md#210-antiderivative-antialiasing-in-passing).
The family resemblance is exact: phase distortion (Casio's CZ line) and FM are the same operation
parameterised differently.

**FM has a modernised branch.** Lazzarini and Timoney's **ModFM** replaces the Bessel functions with
*modified* Bessel functions, which have no oscillating factor and are always non-negative. The
practical consequence is a spectrum that evolves smoothly as the index changes, instead of the
lumpy, formant-hopping behaviour of classic FM at low indices — where a sideband can vanish
entirely because `J_k(I)` passes through zero. If you have ever automated an FM index and heard a
partial disappear and come back, that is `J_k` crossing zero, and ModFM is the fix.

**And in the analog world, linear FM is a rarity worth knowing about.** The CEM3340 of
`research:oscillators/05-machines.md` §5.2 has a linear FM input as well as its
exponential pitch CV, and the distinction matters for exactly the reason §12.2 gives: modulating an
*exponential* pitch input with a bipolar signal raises the mean frequency, because the average of
an exponential is not the exponential of the average. Exponential FM goes sharp as the depth
increases; linear FM does not. It is the analog-domain version of the same integration problem.

## 12.9 Consequences for this repository

mxm-mono-01 has one oscillator and no modulation path, so none of this is implemented. Recorded for the
instrument that wants it:

- **Phase modulation, not frequency modulation.** Same spectra, one failure mode fewer, and it is
  what the hardware did.
- **Sine carriers.** Not tradition — band-limited carriers and phase modulation do not compose.
- **Key-scale the index.** Aliasing scales with pitch, so an index that is safe at A2 is not safe at
  A5. This is cheaper and more effective than any antialiasing after the fact.
- **Watch the −60 dB bandwidth, not the index.** Budget `1.5 × (fc + (I+1)·fm)` and keep it under
  Nyquist. The ratio dominates: at 1:8, index 2 already aliases.
- **2× oversampling if the patch really needs the bandwidth** — it measured 124 dB here, which is
  the one place in this whole reference where oversampling is the efficient answer.
- **Budget 8 ns per operator**, halving to about 4 with a table-read sine.
- **Clamp feedback below about 3**, where the loop goes chaotic, unless chaos is the feature.
